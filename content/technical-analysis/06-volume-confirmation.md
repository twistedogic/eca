# Volume and confirmation

Price tells you what happened; volume tells you how much conviction
stood behind it. This final chapter adds the third column of the chart,
uses it to grade the signals of the previous three chapters, and closes
with the honest limits of the whole topic.

## Reading volume

**Volume** is the number of units traded in a candle — shares, contracts,
lots. Plotted as bars under price, it is the market's attendance record:
high volume means many participants acted, low volume means the move was
made on thin interest.

The base grammar is short:

- Price up on rising volume: the move has participation. Treat it as
  real until proven otherwise.
- Price up on falling volume: fewer and fewer hands are carrying it. The
  direction may hold, but the fuel is running out.
- Volume drying up overall: consolidation. Whoever acts next sets the
  next chapter.

## Volume grades breakouts

The single most practical use: a **breakout** on a **volume spike** —
participation jumping well above its recent average as price closes
beyond a level — is the classic confirmation of chapter 2's loudest
event. A breakout on flat or shrinking volume is the classic fake:
price drifted through a level nobody was watching, and it tends to
wander back.

The mirror signals matter too. A **volume climax** — an enormous spike,
usually at the end of an extended move — is exhaustion: the last
motivated buyers and sellers all acting at once, leaving no one to
continue. And **volume divergence**, price making new highs while volume
shrinks at each one, is the participation quietly leaving the trend —
the same early warning RSI divergence gives, visible in the bars.

> A breakout on a **volume spike** is the market voting with size; a
> breakout that no one showed up for is the market shrugging.

## Context: the higher timeframe

Every signal so far was read on one chart. Practice reads two or three,
from the top down: the daily chart says trend or range — which tactics
are legal; the four-hour or hourly chart carries the level and the
setup; the entry chart carries the trigger candle. A hammer on the
fifteen-minute chart against a daily downtrend is not a signal, it is a
rounding error. The higher timeframe decides what counts; the lower one
only decides when.

## The honest limits

The topic closes where every chapter closed. Technical analysis is
descriptive: levels, patterns, indicators, and volume describe pressure,
participation, and position — a rich map of where the market has been
willing to act. None of it predicts. Pattern studies find modest edge at
best; indicators lag by construction; volume confirms but does not
foresee.

What the framework genuinely buys you is disciplined location: an entry
at a level, confirmed by participation, with the stop beyond the
structure that justified the trade and the target at the next one — all
decided before the trade exists. Whether that setup makes money is
expectancy; whether you keep the money is risk control. The chart's last
job is to keep you honest about both.

> **Volume** is the conviction behind the move — the one column that
> grades every signal price gives you.
