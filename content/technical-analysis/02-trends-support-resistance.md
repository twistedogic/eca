# Trends, support and resistance

Chapter 1 gave you the candle. This chapter gives you the map: the trend
that says which direction to lean, and the levels where price repeatedly
turns. Nearly every method later in this topic — patterns, indicators,
candlestick signals — is an application of these two ideas.

## Trend structure

A **trend** is the direction price has been net-moving over the timeframe
you are reading. Trends are read from swings, not from individual candles:
an **uptrend** is a sequence of higher swing highs and higher swing lows,
a **downtrend** is the mirror of lower highs and lower lows, and a
**range** is what remains when neither side makes progress — rallies die
near one price, dips hold near another.

Why trends matter more than any signal: a mediocre setup traded in the
trend's direction and a brilliant setup traded against it are not
comparable bets. Trend following does not predict; it aligns you with the
side that has been winning.

Trends also end, and structure tells you when: an uptrend survives exactly
as long as each dip holds above the previous dip's low. A close below that
level is the first objective evidence the sequence is broken — everything
before it is noise, and everything after it is a different market.

## Trendlines

A **trendline** is a straight line drawn along the swing lows of an
uptrend (or under the swing highs of a downtrend). It is a hand-drawn
summary of the trend, and it inherits the trend's rule: as long as price
respects the line, the trend is intact; a close through it is a warning.

Treat trendlines as a sketch, not a law. Two analysts drawing the same
trendline routinely disagree, a line that fits perfectly after the fact is
easier to draw than to trade, and every new swing can force a redraw. The
sequence of higher lows is the underlying fact; the line is only its
approximation.

## Support and resistance

A **support** level is a price area where buying has repeatedly stepped in
and stopped declines. A **resistance** level is the mirror — an area where
selling has repeatedly capped rallies. They are not walls but zones: the
memory of where the balance of orders changed hands before, thick enough
that "the level" means the area, not a single tick.

Levels earn strength from their history: the more times an area held, the
more participants watched it hold, and the more orders sit around it.

The most useful property of levels is that they switch jobs. When price
closes decisively through a **resistance** zone, that zone frequently
becomes **support** on the next visit — resistance becomes support, and
support becomes resistance. The reason is arithmetic as much as memory:
traders who sold at the old ceiling are underwater after the break, and
their pain exits are buy orders when price returns.

> When price closes decisively through a level, a **role reversal** often
> follows — old resistance becomes the new support, and old support the
> new resistance.

## Breakouts, and how they fail

A **breakout** is a close beyond a level that had been holding. It is the
market's loudest event, and its most faked one: a pierce that closes back
inside the level — often after sweeping the stops parked just beyond it —
is a **false breakout**, and it is common enough to be a pattern in its
own right.

Two habits separate real from fake better than any indicator:

- Judge breaks on closes, not wicks. A wick beyond a level is a probe; a
  body closing beyond it is a decision.
- Demand participation. A breakout on expanding volume and follow-through
  candles is a migration; a breakout on shrinking volume into a quiet
  session is usually just the stops.

The disciplined response to either outcome is the same shape: a
**pullback** entry waits for the break, then enters near the level on the
retest, with the stop tight beyond it. You trade fewer moves this way, and
you pay for far fewer fakes.

## Stops live at levels

Levels give the stop a logical home: the trade's reason lives at the
level, so the stop belongs beyond it. Buying a bounce at support means the
thesis dies on a close below the zone — that is where the exit goes, not
at a distance chosen for comfort. A stop that ignores the structure is a
scheduled loss; a stop beyond the level is a decision.

> A **stop** placed beyond the level that justified the trade is the only
> placement where being wrong is cheap and unambiguous.

And the standing limit from chapter 1: levels describe where balances
changed before. They tell you where to be wrong cheaply — never where
price must go next.
