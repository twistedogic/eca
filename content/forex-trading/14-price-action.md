# Price action trading

Chapter 9 introduced technical analysis and its weakness: every indicator is
a delayed, smoothed derivative of price. **Price action** trading drops the
middleman: it is the practice of deciding from the raw record of price — the
sequence of opens, highs, lows, and closes — with levels drawn on the chart
and little else.

The argument for it is short. A moving average, an RSI, a MACD are all
arithmetic on four numbers per candle. If the information is in those
numbers, the indicator can only ever be a lagging summary of them. Read the
original.

That is also the honest limit of the method, and this chapter returns to it
at the end: price action shows what has already happened more clearly than
the indicators do, but it predicts nothing on its own.

## Market structure

Price action starts with the skeleton of the chart. A **swing high** is a
local peak: a candle whose high stands above the highs of the candles either
side of it. A **swing low** is the mirror, a local trough. The sequence of
swings is the **market structure**, and it is the only objective definition
of trend the market offers.

- An uptrend is a sequence of higher highs and higher lows — each rally
  clears the last, each dip holds above the previous dip.
- A downtrend is the mirror: lower highs and lower lows.
- When swings overlap, rallies failing near one price and dips holding near
  another, the market is ranging, and range tactics apply instead of trend
  tactics.

The structure, not an oscillator, says when the trend is over. A **break of
structure** is a close beyond the most recent swing against the trend — in
an uptrend, a close below the last higher low. Until that happens, the map
says buy the dips; after it happens, the trend thesis is dead and the exit
is not optional.

> A **break of structure** — in an uptrend, a close below the last higher
> low — is the first objective evidence that the trend has ended.

Judge breaks on closes, not wicks. Wicks trade beyond swings constantly; a
body closing beyond a swing is a decision, a wick poking past it is a probe.

## Reading a single candle

A candle compresses a fight into four numbers. The body, open to close, is
who won the interval. The **wick** — the thin marks above and below — is the
ground each side probed and gave back.

Three candle shapes carry most of the weight in forex:

- A **pin bar** has a long wick on one side and a small body at the other
  end. Price probed a level and was pushed back, so the wick records
  rejection. The same candle in the middle of a range records nothing.
- An **engulfing candle** has a body that swallows the previous candle's
  body entirely: initiative changed hands at that price, it did not just
  drift.
- An **inside bar** trades entirely within the previous candle's range.
  Volatility is contracting while both sides wait; the tradeable event is
  the break of that range, not the bar itself.

> A **pin bar** records rejection — price visited a level and was pushed
> away — not a promise that the level will hold.

Be honest with yourself about this evidence. Tested standalone, candlestick
patterns mostly fail. Marshall, Young, and Rose's bootstrap test of
candlestick rules on Dow stocks (Journal of Banking & Finance, 2006) found
no value once costs were paid, and follow-up work on Japanese and Thai
equities and on currency futures agrees: at best a few rules reach
significance, none reliably. The candle's job is confirmation: a pin bar at
the third test of a level that
aligns with the session low is evidence; the same shape at a random price
is a coin flip with better marketing.

## Levels and liquidity

Chapter 9 defined support and resistance as areas where the balance of
orders has changed hands before. Price action explains why those areas
persist: memory and pain.

A broken level commonly flips roles, a **role reversal**. Resistance that
breaks and holds becomes support, because the traders who sold the break are
now underwater and act — cutting losses or finally buying — when price
returns to the scene.

The less comfortable half is **liquidity**. Stops cluster just beyond the
obvious places: above swing highs, below swing lows, at round numbers, at
the previous day's high and low. Resting stops are working orders, and
anyone who needs size filled needs counterparty orders. A **false
breakout** is what it looks like when they get used: price pushes beyond the
obvious level, triggers the stops, fills a large order against them, and
closes back inside — a pierce of the swing that never closes beyond it,
often labelled a **swing failure pattern**. The spike was fuel, not a
signal.

> A **false breakout** spikes beyond the level, fills the resting stops, and
> closes back inside — the level was fuel, not a signal.

Session mechanics from chapter 4 matter here. The London and New York opens
produce most of the deliberate stop runs of the day; a sweep of the Asian
range's high or low shortly after London opens is common enough that trading
the first break of the session is usually donating to it.

## Breakout or pullback

Every discretionary entry is one of two shapes.

The **breakout entry** is a stop order placed beyond the level, from chapter
5: if the level goes, you are in. It guarantees a fill on every real move
and pays for that by taking every false breakout at full price.

The **pullback entry** waits. Let the level break, then enter on the retest,
near the level, in the direction of the break. The entry sits closer to the
invalidating swing, so the stop is tighter and the same target pays a larger
multiple of risk. The cost is real: the strongest moves never come back, and
a retest that never happens is a normal outcome, not an anomaly.

> The **breakout entry** guarantees you a position, not a move; the
> **pullback entry** offers the better price and no guarantee of a fill.

Match the entry to the structure. In an extended, one-sided trend, waiting
for pullbacks means waiting forever, and breakouts pay. In chop, the
breakout entry is a subscription to stop hunts, and the pullback is what
keeps you out of them.

## Where the risk lives

Price action gives the stop a home: beyond the structure that made the trade
worth taking. Buying a dip that held above the last swing low means the
**invalidation level** is a close below that low — there the reason for the
trade is objectively wrong, so that is where the stop belongs, with room for
the noise. The ATR from chapter 9 measures that noise; a stop tighter than
an average candle is not a stop, it is a scheduled loss.

The rest is chapter 8's arithmetic, and it is not negotiable. Size from the
stop distance, never the other way round. If the only stop that respects the
structure is too wide for 1% risk at the minimum position size, the trade
does not exist. Targets go at the next opposing structure or at a stated R
multiple, and in an established trend a trailing stop (chapter 6) ratchets
behind each new higher low.

## Context, and the honest limits

**Top-down analysis** reads the chart from above: the daily structure says
trend or range and therefore which tactics are legal; the hourly chart gives
the level and the setup; the five-minute chart gives the trigger candle.
Trading the trigger without the context is how pin bars end up traded in the
middle of nowhere — the exact mistake the confirmation paragraph warned
about.

And the limits, plainly. Price action is descriptive, like all technical
analysis, and its patterns add no certainty when tested standalone. What the
framework genuinely buys you is disciplined location: an entry at a level, a
stop beyond the structure, a target at the next one, all decided before the
trade. Whether that setup makes money is still expectancy, chapter 10, and
whether you keep the money is still risk control, chapter 8.

> **Price action** tells you where to be wrong cheaply; it never tells you
> where price is going.

## Market events to watch

Price action reads order flow, and the largest orders of the week arrive on
a schedule. The **economic calendar** is therefore part of the setup, not
background: before the session, know which high-impact releases land today,
on which currency, at what time. Calendars grade impact from low to high;
the high-impact tier is the one that redraws charts.

A few events do most of the damage, and they are the same ones chapter 9
named as the sharpest moves of the week:

- Central bank **rate decisions** — the decision, then the statement and
  press conference that follow, which routinely move the currency more than
  the headline number does.
- Inflation prints, above all US CPI, currently the single biggest
  scheduled mover.
- US employment, above all non-farm payrolls on the first Friday.
- The second tier: PMIs, GDP, retail sales — real moves, smaller teeth.
- **Intervention**, the one unscheduled entry: a central bank (in practice,
  usually the Bank of Japan) buying or selling its own currency directly.
  It is rare, and it runs through stops like nothing on the calendar.

The price action around a release follows a shape worth knowing. In the
hour before, spreads widen and price compresses into the nearest level as
both sides wait. At the release, the spike is frequently the day's
liquidity event — the sweep of the session's obvious high or low happens in
the first seconds, exactly the false-breakout sequence from earlier in this
chapter. And the first move is not the verdict: a **whipsaw** within
minutes, the spike reversing as fast money repositions against the trapped
late entries, is a normal outcome, not an anomaly.

Three rules follow. No working stops parked into a release unless the trade
thesis is the release itself (a different style, chapter 10) — event risk
and widened spreads go through stops. No entries in the first minutes; let
the impulse spend itself and trade the first pullback, which is the pullback
entry applied to news. And treat pre-release levels as obsolete: redraw the
structure from the release candle, because the orders that made yesterday's
levels were just consumed.

> A scheduled release does not just move price — it resets the
> **market structure** you were reading.
