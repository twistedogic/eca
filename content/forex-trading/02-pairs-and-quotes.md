# Pairs and quotes

A currency pair is named by writing the base currency first and the quote
currency second. EUR/USD = 1.0850 means that one euro buys 1.0850 US
dollars. USD/JPY = 150.00 means one US dollar buys 150 Japanese yen. The
pair is always read as "units of quote currency per unit of base currency".

## Which pairs to trade

The market divides into three tiers, and the tier tells you how much
liquidity, how tight a spread, and how much volume you can expect.

- **Majors** are the pairs involving the US dollar against a major
  currency: EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD, AUD/USD, USD/CHF, USD/CAD,
  NZD/USD. These carry the deepest liquidity and the tightest spreads.
- **Minors**, also called **crosses**, omit the dollar: EUR/JPY,
  GBP/CHF, AUD/CAD. Liquidity is thinner than the majors but still solid.
- **Exotics** pair a major currency with a small or emerging-market
  currency: USD/TRY, EUR/ZAR, USD/MXN. Spreads are wide, gaps are common,
  and they cost materially more to trade.

> **Majors** carry the deepest liquidity and the tightest spreads; **exotics**
> are wide, expensive, and jumpy.

If you are learning, trade the majors. A tight spread on EUR/USD buys you
room that a wide spread on an exotic pair spends before the trade even
works.

## Reading a quote

Dealers quote two prices at the same moment. The **bid** is the price at
which the dealer buys your base currency — the price you hit when you sell.
The **ask** is the price at which the dealer sells it to you — the price you
pay when you buy. The difference between them is the **spread**, quoted in
pips.

> The **bid** is the price you sell at; the **ask** is the price you buy at.
> The gap between them is the **spread**.

The spread is not a separate fee. It is built into the price you trade
against: you buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so one round turn costs you
exactly one spread. A 1.0 pip spread on one standard lot of EUR/USD is
therefore about $10, before any commission.

## One contract, two currencies

Buying EUR/USD is simultaneously buying euros and selling dollars. That
double exposure is why correlation matters: a short EUR/USD and a long
USD/CHF are both dollar-short positions wearing different clothes. Chapter
7 covers how to stop two "different" trades from being one large trade.
