# Entries and the trader's equation

An entry is not a prediction. It is the moment analysis becomes risk,
and it has a gate that outranks every signal: before any trade, the
expected reward must clear the expected loss. This chapter is about
that gate and the entry tactics that respect it.

## Two independent reasons

Never enter on a single reason. Any two *independent* reasons — a
pullback to a moving average that is also a swing-low support in a
higher-timeframe uptrend — are the minimum. There are only three cases
where one reason suffices: a strong trend, a trend-line overshoot with
a good reversal bar, and a second entry, where the first attempt was
the other reason by definition.

Independence is the whole point. Two moving averages crossing is one
fact computed from the same prices twice. Two independent systems
agreeing — trend alignment plus a rejection tail at a level — is two
facts. Weight the strong facts higher: trend alignment on the higher
timeframe, a rejection tail exactly at the level, a second attempt at
a pullback.

## The trader's equation

The gate every entry must pass:

```
probability × reward  >  (1 − probability) × risk
```

The break-even reward depends on the win rate:

| Win rate | Reward needed to break even |
|---|---|
| 70% | 0.5× risk |
| 60% | 1.0× risk |
| 50% | 2.0× risk |
| 40% | 3.0× risk |

> Never take a trade expecting to make less than you risk — that rule
> survives every level of uncertainty about the **win rate**.

If you are uncertain about the probability, assume 50%. If the setup
looks good, assume 60%. Don't agonise over the exact number; the reward
floor is the point. And note that all three variables — probability,
reward, risk — change on every tick as the trade develops, which is
why trade management (chapter 16) is part of the entry decision, not an
afterthought.

## Directional probability hovers near 50%

The chance that an equidistant move goes your way sits around 50%
almost all day. It rises briefly at the edges of well-defined ranges
and during strong spikes — and those edges are fleeting. As soon as an
edge becomes consensus, the market neutralises it.

The practical consequence: be fast when you have an edge, then leave.
Once the trade is on, do not wait for a better price — the market
rarely volunteers one, and the edge decays while you wait.

## Enter on stops, not limits

Use stop orders to enter, not limit orders. The difference is not the
fill price — it is what the fill tells you.

A limit entry is filled *because* the market moved against you: you
start the trade losing, buying into a falling market. A stop entry is
filled because the market moved *with* you, and the position is nearly
always in profit immediately. Entering on strength is the wind at your
back; entering on a limit is fighting the market before the trade has
begun.

> A **failed signal is a signal**: when a clean setup breaks the wrong
> way, the market has told you something — exit and reverse in one
> order.

The one exception is size: an order large enough to move the market
itself has to be worked with limits. Retail size is not that order.

## Pattern high, entry low

Find the setup on the higher timeframe — daily or 4-hour — and execute
on the lower one, hourly down to 15 minutes. The stop distance
collapses: an 80-pip stop on the daily setup may become a 20-pip stop
on the lower-timeframe entry, which means a larger position for the
same percentage risk.

Two limits keep this honest. Lower timeframes are for entry refinement,
never for standalone analysis — a 15-minute chart analysed alone is
noise. And below 5 minutes, noise dominates at any skill level; there
is no signal fast enough to pay the spread.

## Confirmation costs less than it saves

Requiring a second, independent indicator to agree with the signal
before entering — ADX rising alongside a breakout, momentum confirming
a reversal — removes losing trades at the cost of occasionally missing
a winner. That trade is usually worth making: the losses you skip are
certain, the winners you miss are occasional.
