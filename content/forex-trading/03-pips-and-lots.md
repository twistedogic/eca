# Pips and lots

Positions in forex are sized in **lots**, and price moves are measured in
**pips**. Both conventions exist so that risk can be calculated in money
before a trade is placed.

## The pip

A **pip** is the fourth decimal place of most currency pairs, or 0.0001.
For pairs quoted in Japanese yen it is the second decimal place, 0.01,
because the yen is worth far less than the dollar.

A **pipette**, sometimes called a point, is the last decimal place —
0.00001, or 0.001 for yen pairs. Brokers quote prices in pipettes and
count costs in pips, which is why an advertised "0.3 pip" spread is really
3 pipettes on a non-yen pair.

> A **pip** is 0.0001 on most pairs and 0.01 on yen pairs; a **pipette** is
> one decimal place finer.

## Lot size and pip value

A **standard lot** is 100,000 units of the base currency. A **mini lot** is
10,000 units, a **micro lot** is 1,000 units, and a nano lot is 100 units.
For any pair where the dollar is the quote currency — EUR/USD, GBP/USD,
AUD/USD, NZD/USD — one pip on a standard lot is worth exactly $10.

The pip value scales down with the lot: $1 on a mini lot, $0.10 on a micro
lot. For a pair where the dollar is the base currency, such as USD/JPY, the
pip value depends on the exchange rate: at USD/JPY 150.00, one pip on a
standard lot is about $6.67, not $10.

> On EUR/USD a standard lot moves **$10** per pip; the same position in a
> mini lot moves $1 and in a micro lot $0.10.

## Why the units matter

Lot size and pip value together convert price movement into money, which is
the only thing that matters for risk. A 50-pip move is $500 on a standard
lot, $50 on a mini lot, and $5 on a micro lot — the same percentage move,
one tenth of the money each time down.

Because pip value is fixed by convention and not by your account size,
position sizing is arithmetic rather than guesswork. Chapter 8 turns these
numbers into a position size.

> A 50-pip move is $500 on a **standard lot**, $50 on a mini lot, and $5 on
> a micro lot.
