# Regime and the value area

Chapter 17 gated each entry on its own maths. This chapter gates the
whole session: before any setup is even looked for, ask what regime the
market is in. Traders who ignored their own higher-timeframe filter
have lost eight-figure sums in a single year — the filter is not
decoration.

## The value area

Over any lookback window, plot where trading actually happened. The
**value area** is the range where the bulk of it occurred — the prices
the market accepted, spending real time there rather than passing
through. Price inside value is agreed; price outside value is
contested.

Value, not price, defines the regime. When the value area itself steps
higher day over day, that is a trend — the market is accepting
progressively higher prices. When value sits still, price poking above
it is a probe, not a breakout.

## Tails, excess, and control price

Three markers inside and around the value area do most of the work:

- A **tail** — a single-bar spike out of value that closes back inside —
  is the strongest rejection signal the market prints.
- **Excess price** — brief excursions beyond value — is where
  institutions left their footprints, and therefore the strongest
  support and resistance.
- The **control price** — the pivot inside the value area where the
  most volume changed hands — is the magnet. Price drawn toward it is
  the default; escaping it is the signal.

## How a regime ends

A trend does not end with a headline; it ends on the chart, in one of
three ways:

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rotations narrow into the wrong half of value | Sellers are accepting higher prices — the trend is exhausting |
| A tail appears on the opposing side | The strongest rejection print, against you |
| Price travels excessive distance beyond value | Counter-interest is being drawn in |

> The higher-timeframe trend says which side you are on; if it is
> against you, don't trade the setup — or size it right down in
> **regime** terms, not in pips.

One regime signal is not on the currency chart at all: your own equity
curve. A downside break in your paper equity means your read on the
regime is wrong, whatever the chart says. Stand aside or trade smaller
until the equity curve makes a new high.

## Thin liquidity moves violently

Chapter 4 mapped the sessions. The missing piece is what thin liquidity
does to price: forced-covering cascades. If enough shorts were built on
a rally, further gains force some to buy back; with few counterparties
around, price jumps abruptly, provoking a flood of similar buying from
the remaining shorts. Thin markets are quiet until they are violent.

The practical consequences: in the Asia-Pacific session, the New York
afternoon, and holiday periods, widen the stop margin of error, cut the
size, and usually decline level-dependent setups altogether. Holidays
are lower-volatility until aggressive speculators exploit the thin book
to push price past a key technical point.

## Each pair has habits

| Pair | Trait | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| EUR/USD | Deepest liquidity; fewest price jumps | Tightest spreads, fewest false breaks |
| EUR/USD | Backing and filling — retraces more of short-term moves than other pairs | The *absence* of retracement is a stronger, more genuine move |
| GBP/USD, USD/CHF | Leading indicators for EUR/USD at equivalent levels | If they are testing a level, EUR/USD likely follows — seconds to days ahead |
| USD/JPY | An on/off switch: narrow ranges, then sudden marches; tracks US yields | Respect the switch; a >5bp yield move deserves attention |
| GBP, JPY pairs | The most illiquid, volatile majors | Most prone to false breaks; widest stop margin needed |
| All pairs | Round numbers cluster stops and option strikes | The red zone about 20 pips around a round handle has gravity |

## Confirm the dollar across markets

The dollar's move can be cross-checked before you trust it. Dollar up,
US 10-year yields up, gold down — that is confirmation of dollar
strength. Dollar up while yields are flat and gold rises — the move is
suspect. Oil adds an asymmetric tell: it gains more on a falling dollar
than it loses on a rising one.
