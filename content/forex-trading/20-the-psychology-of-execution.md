# The psychology of execution

Chapter 11 catalogued the behavioural traps. This chapter is about the
machinery that defeats them: the insight underneath the traps, the way
to grade trades so the traps stop paying, and the order in which to
install defences so that willpower is the last line, not the first.

## One mechanism, two failures

The traps are not many — they are one. When your ego is wrapped up in
being right, you hold losers too long *and* cut winners too early. The
two classic failure modes are the same mechanism seen from each end:
the ego cannot afford to book the loss, and it cannot risk giving back
the win.

> Stop evaluating trades by whether they made money. A **rule-following
> loss is a good decision**; a rule-breaking win is a bad one.

Hope is the same fault on the losing side: the loser is held while
waiting for it to come back. Every countermeasure in this chapter
exists to make that decision mechanically, before the ego is consulted.

## Grade the decision, not the outcome

Grade every trade on two independent axes. The decision grade: did the
thesis hold up, were the rules followed, was the size right? The
outcome: the R multiple, from chapter 8. A rule-following loss is a
good decision. A rule-breaking win is a bad one.

Most journals record only the outcome — and therefore teach exactly the
wrong lesson, reinforcing whatever happened to pay that week. The
two-axis grade is the correction, and it only works if the thesis was
written down *before* the entry and frozen.

## The cost of a bad trade

A bad trade costs more than its loss. The real damage is the next good
trade you don't take: you are shaken, you hesitate, and the move
happens without you. Missed trades cost roughly as much as realised
losses, and no journal line ever shows them.

The matching countermeasure is patience that runs both ways: wait for
the right trade, and log the ones you pass on — including whether they
would have worked. That log is the only way to make patience measurable
instead of a mood.

## No profit targets

Never set a profit target — not daily, not monthly. A monthly number
forces marginal trades at month-end to hit it, and a common trait among
failed traders was exactly that goal. Opportunities arrive sporadically;
the target converts their absence into a personal failure.

Set process goals instead: "exit at the stop without hesitating",
"log the thesis before every entry". Focus on what each trade taught
you, not what it paid. The pressure to improve is a healthier pressure
than the pressure to make this month's number.

## Think in probabilities

"I'm going to make money on this trade" creates stress the moment it
fails. "I'll probably lose money on this trade, but I'll make money on
my trading" creates almost none — and is closer to the truth at any
realistic win rate.

Be the casino, not the gambler. The casino owner does not flinch at a
losing spin; he deals again, because the edge is in the repetition, not
the single outcome. When uncertain, ask what the casino owner would do.

## Know your real motive

Money is usually a minor reason people trade. Action, the puzzle,
validation, image, the hope of a quick score — each motive produces a
different failure mode, and each is served by a different system. Your
motive should shape the system you pick, because the system you will
actually execute is the one that fits your motive. A daily pre-session
check-in — focus, energy, ego, FOMO — completes the picture: if ego or
FOMO reads high, don't arm new positions that day.

## The discipline ladder

Install the defences in this order, because the first five need no
willpower at all:

1. Hard stops in the market — mechanical.
2. Position sizing from the stop — mechanical.
3. Risk tiers with automatic size reduction — mechanical.
4. A penalty box on drawdown — mechanical.
5. A journal: pre-trade thesis, post-mortem grading — semi-mechanical.
6. Never average down; no profit targets — decision rules.
7. The emotional check-in — human.

> Relying on willpower in trading is like a recovering alcoholic keeping
> a bottle of whiskey on the kitchen counter — the **discipline ladder**
> removes the bottle instead of testing you against it.

The mechanical layers come first precisely so the human layer is asked
for so little. Overconfidence after a hot run — the largest losses
follow the best runs — is handled the same way: reduce size
automatically after a strong stretch, just as after a losing one.
