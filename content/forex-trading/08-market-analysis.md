# Market analysis

Two families of analysis ask different questions. **Technical analysis**
asks what price has already done. **Fundamental analysis** asks what is
driving the economy behind the price. Most retail traders pick one and
ignore the other, and most of their losses come from the analysis, not
from the execution.

## Technical analysis

The core idea of **technical analysis** is that prices move in trends and
those trends leave repeated patterns, because participants respond to the
same levels and the same information.

- **Support and resistance** are price levels where orders cluster.
  Support is where buyers previously stepped in; resistance is where
  sellers previously stepped in. They are not walls — they are areas where
  the balance of orders has repeatedly changed hands.
- The **moving average** smooths price into a trend filter. The common set
  is 20, 50, and 200 periods, with the 200 acting as the long-term
  dividing line between uptrend and downtrend.
- The **RSI** (relative strength index) measures momentum on a 0–100
  scale over 14 periods. Above 70 is conventionally called overbought,
  below 30 oversold — though in a strong trend RSI can sit at extremes for
  a long time.
- The **MACD** is the gap between a 12-period and a 26-period exponential
  moving average, smoothed by a 9-period signal line. It shows whether
  momentum is accelerating or fading.
- The **ATR** (average true range, 14 periods) measures volatility in
  price units. It is what tells you whether a stop distance is sensible.

> The **RSI** uses 14 periods and the **MACD** 12, 26, and 9 — the standard
> settings, not opinions.

Technical analysis is descriptive, not predictive. Its weakness is that it
has no way to distinguish a genuine breakout from a false one until price
has already moved.

## Fundamental analysis

**Fundamental analysis** looks at the macroeconomic forces that move
currencies. The workhorse is the **interest rate differential**: when one
country's policy rate is far above another's, holding the higher-yielding
currency pays a return.

That payment is the **carry trade**. Buy the high-yield currency against
the low-yielding one, and the interest difference accrues daily as
rollover — the mechanism from chapter 4. The trade earns money while the
exchange rate stays put, and loses badly when it moves against you. Carry
positions tend to be crowded, which is exactly why they unwind violently
when central banks surprise.

> The **carry trade** earns the **interest rate differential** as rollover
> while the exchange rate stays put — and loses badly when it moves.

The other driver is **central bank policy**. Rate decisions, and the
language around them, move rates and therefore currencies. A hawkish
surprise — tighter than expected — tends to lift a currency; a dovish
surprise tends to weaken it. This is why scheduled data releases matter:
**CPI**, **non-farm payrolls**, and policy statements routinely produce
the sharpest moves of the week.

## Combining them

A workable split is to use fundamentals for the direction and the
technical picture for the timing. "The rate differential favours the dollar
and the chart confirms the downtrend" is a thesis. "The chart looks
overbought" is not a thesis, because overbought can persist for months in
a trending market.

Whichever you use, write the reason down before the trade. Chapter 9
covers why that matters.
