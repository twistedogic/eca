# What is forex

**Foreign exchange** is the trading of one currency against another. It is
the largest market in the world by traded volume, and it is where currency
conversion happens for most international commerce — invoices, remittances,
and the pricing of assets denominated in other currencies.

Every trade has two sides. A **currency pair** such as EUR/USD is always
quoted as two currencies, and the first one, the **base currency**, is the
unit being bought or sold. The second, the **quote currency**, is the unit
the price is expressed in.

## How the market is built

There is no central exchange and no central clearing house for the retail
**spot market**, the market for buying and selling currencies for immediate
delivery. Instead the market is **over-the-counter**: dealers quote to each
other over a network of electronic platforms and voice lines, and each dealer
passes those prices on to its own clients.

Because there is no single order book, the market is a **decentralised
market**. The same currency pair trades at slightly different prices at
different dealers at the same moment, and no price is "the" price of
EUR/USD.

> The **spot market** has no central exchange; dealers quote to each other
> and pass those quotes on to their own clients.

## How big it is

The Bank for International Settlements runs a triennial survey of global
foreign exchange activity. The April 2025 survey recorded average daily
turnover of $9.6 trillion, up 28% from $7.5 trillion in 2022. The
seven most-traded pairs accounted for about 66% of that total, and foreign
exchange swaps remained the single largest instrument.

Almost all of that turnover is wholesale. The retail slice — you and me —
is a rounding error in the notional volume, which is exactly why retail
traders compete for the same liquidity, the same spreads, and the same
news.

## A realistic starting point

Read this before the rest of the course. According to the CFTC's own
published data, about two out of three retail forex traders end each
quarter in the red. European regulator ESMA puts the share of losing
retail CFD accounts in a similar range, at 74% to 89%.

> About two out of three **retail forex traders** end each quarter in the
> red, according to the CFTC.

Those numbers are not a warning against trading. They are a description of
what the activity is: a business where most participants lose and a small
minority profit, usually because they treat risk as a process rather than
as a feeling. Everything in this course is built around that: define the
risk first, then take the trade.
