# Taxes and reporting

A profit on a screen is not money in your pocket, and the gap between the
two is where a lot of unpleasant surprises live. This chapter explains how
forex profit is treated, because the answer is not the same in every
country and the wrong regime can cost more than a bad trade.

> This chapter is general education, **not tax advice**. Tax rules differ by
> country, change regularly, and turn on facts only a professional can
> check. Use it to ask better questions, not to file a return.

## The first question: business or hobby

Before any rate or threshold, every tax authority asks the same question:
are you **trading as a business**, or investing as a private individual?

The answer changes almost everything. Business traders are taxed on profit
as income, can deduct their trading costs, and can usually offset losses
against other income. Private investors are taxed under capital gains
rules, usually get an annual exemption, and often cannot deduct losses
against salary or other income at all.

> Where the **business or hobby** line falls can be worth more than any
> individual trade you will ever take.

Which side you fall on is decided by facts, not by choice: how often you
trade, the size and regularity of your positions, whether you run it as a
business, whether you keep records, and whether your intent is profit
rather than capital appreciation. Get this right early — it is hard to
recharacterise three years of trading afterwards.

## What the broker reports, and what you report

For retail spot forex, most brokers report little or nothing to the tax
authority, and some report nothing at all. Where you trade an
exchange-traded product, the broker may report the numbers for you — in
the US, for instance, a broker handling 1256 contracts files Form 6781 on
your behalf.

If the broker does not report, you do. That means keeping a record of
every trade: date and time, pair, direction, size, entry, exit, realised
profit or loss, and the swap or interest charged. Add the currency
conversion rate you used if your account currency is not your home
currency.

> If the broker does not report your trading, you do — and **the record**
> has to exist before you need it.

Reconstructing a year of trades from chat messages and statements at tax
time is miserable. The journal from chapter 9 already contains most of
what you need; add the swap column and you are done.

## Cash or mark-to-market

Some regimes tax a position when you close it. Others tax it as if you had
closed it at the end of the year. The second approach is called
**mark-to-market**: any open position on the last trading day of the year
is treated as sold at that day's settlement price and immediately rebought,
so unrealised gains are taxable and unrealised losses are deductible, and
your cost basis resets to the year-end price. It also makes annual
reporting simpler, because the tax year closes itself.

> **Mark-to-market** means tax can be due on a gain you never took in cash.

In the US this distinction is concrete: retail spot forex is taxed by
default as **ordinary income**, so losses offset all of your other income
with no annual cap, while currency futures traded on a regulated exchange
qualify for 60/40 long-term and short-term capital gains treatment with
mark-to-market accounting.

## A short guide by jurisdiction

Each of these summaries is a starting point for a conversation with an
adviser, not a substitute for one.

- United States. Retail spot forex defaults to ordinary income or
  ordinary loss. Currency futures on a qualified exchange get the 60/40
  split and mark-to-market, which means capital losses subject to the
  annual limit on capital losses against ordinary income. The **wash sale
  rule** does not apply to forex, so you can take a loss and re-enter the
  same pair immediately without the loss being disallowed. Separately,
  **foreign account reporting** rules can require you to disclose accounts
  held with brokers in other countries — in the US, the FBAR threshold is
  $10,000 in aggregate at any point in the year, and it is filed with
  FinCEN rather than the IRS. State tax applies on top of federal.
- United Kingdom. Two regimes, and the instrument decides which one
  you are in. Spread betting is treated as gambling, so profits are outside
  the tax net — but losses are not deductible either. CFDs and spot forex
  sit in the capital gains regime, with an **annual exempt amount** of
  £3,000 for 2026/27. If the activity amounts to a trade rather than
  investment, it is income tax instead, with National Insurance. Exceptions
  include hedging a business exposure and trading through a company, which
  pays corporation tax. None of these products qualify for an ISA or SIPP.
- Germany. Private speculative trading generally falls under the
  capital gains regime at a flat Abgeltungsteuer of 26.375%, with no
  holding-period relief. The 2024 Annual Tax Act removed the old €20,000
  annual cap on offsetting derivative losses, so forex, CFD and futures
  losses can now offset without that limit. Trading as a business moves
  you onto income tax with deductible losses. German rules are among the
  most intricate here — get advice before you rely on any of this.
- Japan. FX through a Japanese broker is taxed under separate
  self-assessment at a flat 20.315% (15% national, 5% resident, 0.315%
  reconstruction) rather than at marginal rates, so it is not combined with
  your salary; losses can be carried forward three years, but only if you
  file. Overseas brokers are taxed comprehensively as miscellaneous income
  at marginal rates, with no loss carryforward. A return is required once
  annual profit exceeds ¥200,000 for a salaried person or ¥480,000
  otherwise.
- Australia. The ATO generally treats active forex trading as carrying
  on a business, so profits are assessed as ordinary income at marginal
  rates and losses are deductible in the same year. Casual or investor
  traders may instead fall under the capital gains rules. All worldwide
  income must be reported, whatever country the broker is in.

## The traps

- **Interest income** is not trading profit. Carry and rollover earnings
  are usually taxed differently from capital gains, and can pull you into
  a regime you did not expect.
- Currency conversion changes the number. Your tax is computed in your
  home currency, and the rate applied can move it materially.
- Losses are not always deductible, and the rules for offsetting them
  change with your tax year.
- Trader status has knock-on effects — self-employment contributions,
  benefit and pension treatment, mortgage questions — that are a
  professional consultation, not a checklist item.
- Reporting is separate from paying. A jurisdiction can require you to
  disclose foreign accounts regardless of whether you owe tax on them.

## What to do

Find out which regime you are in before you scale, not after. Keep the
per-trade record from day one. And once you are trading real money at a
size where the answer matters, spend an hour with an adviser in your own
jurisdiction — it is much cheaper than a dispute.
